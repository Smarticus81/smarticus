Teachers repeatedly described Atticus as capable, curious, polite, engaged, and able to learn concepts quickly. Specific observations included:
- Mathematics: strong mathematical abilities; prior Grade 5 work included powers/exponents, order of operations, single-step equations, decimals, graphs, and fraction operations. Incomplete or late work sometimes reduced performance.
- French: teacher described a clear gift for language and expected continued French study in Grade 6. Grade fluctuations were linked partly to missing/incomplete work rather than lack of ability.
- Science: strong curiosity, quick concept acquisition, confidence with investigations, and creative research work. Teacher encouraged fuller completion and refinement.
- Humanities/reading/writing: comprehension and participation were often stronger than grades suggested. Teachers emphasized reading carefully, completing vocabulary/reading assignments, completing all stages of the writing process, asking for clarification, and revising from feedback.

## Longitudinal instructional implications
1. Do not infer mastery solely from historical grades; check current work.
2. Do not lower intellectual expectations merely because completion has been inconsistent.
3. Explicitly teach assignment-completion and self-check habits.
4. In reading, monitor rushing and require evidence-based responses.
5. In writing, require complete process: plan, draft, respond to feedback, revise, proofread.
6. In mathematics, use Grade 6 instruction from the beginning and accelerate only after current mastery is demonstrated.
7. Continue French rather than restarting as a beginner.
8. Preserve hands-on, investigative science and creative/project-based work because these have historically been areas of engagement.
