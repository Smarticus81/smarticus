# From Mountains and Harbors to the Polis

Ancient Greece did not develop as one large river-valley kingdom. Mountains divided much of the mainland into smaller regions, while the coastline opened toward the Aegean and Mediterranean Seas. This combination encouraged communities to grow separately on land while still connecting by water.

Many Greek communities developed as poleis (POH-lays), the plural of polis. A polis was more than a crowded town. It included a city or central settlement, nearby farmland, and the people who belonged to the community. Each polis could make its own laws, organize its own government, and defend its own territory.

Sea travel helped reduce some of the isolation created by mountains. Harbors allowed ships to carry pottery, olive oil, wine, metals, grain, people, and ideas. Fishing also supplied food. Trade became especially important because fertile farmland was limited in many areas. Communities could obtain resources they did not produce easily themselves.

Greek poleis were often politically independent, but they were not completely disconnected from one another. Many Greeks shared versions of the same language, worshiped many of the same gods, told common myths, and participated in religious festivals and athletic contests. In other words, they could be culturally connected without being politically unified under one ruler.

That combination - local independence plus wider cultural connections - became an important feature of the Greek world. It also created competition. Poleis could trade with one another, cooperate against an outside threat, or become rivals. Later in the course, Athens and Sparta will show how two Greek city-states could share important parts of Greek culture while developing very different political and social systems.
